# Copyright-Safe Reuse: Borrowing Without Stealing

> **DISCLAIMER — EDUCATION ONLY, NEVER MEDICAL ADVICE.** This chapter is
> general education about copyright and patient-education materials; it is not
> legal advice, and for anything beyond your facility's own templates, your
> facility's copyright journey is guided by their counsel. **Never put patient
> data into any AI tool.**

## The rule of thumb

Copyright applies to **expression** — the specific words, pictures, and
layout — not to **ideas, facts, or medical knowledge**. You may always:

- Learn from any handout and write your own version of its information.
- Have an AI rewrite **your own** draft, in your own words, all day long.
- Use anything your facility owns, in the ways it allows.

You may not (without permission or a license allowing reuse):

- Copy another organization's patient-education sheet, or substantial wordings
  from it, into your facility's documents.
- Copy their illustrations, layout, or branding.
- Scan their handout and ask an AI to "reword this so we can print it" — that
  is copying with extra steps.

"Adapt, don't copy" is the whole chapter in three words.

## Why "AI rewording a source" is not a loophole

It is tempting: a competitor's handout is perfect, so you feed it to a model
and ask for a rewrite. Two problems:

1. **It can still be infringement.** A machine paraphrase of a protected work
   keeps its selection, structure, and order — and paraphrase of expression can
   infringe regardless of who (or what) reworded it.
2. **It hides the source.** Now nobody can audit where a claim came from, which
   breaks chapter 05's source-tracing check and makes future updates harder.

The safe version achieves the same outcome with no risk:

```text
Use the source as a checklist, not a script. From the material, list the
TOPICS covered (e.g., wound care, activity limits, signs of infection).
Close it. Then draft YOUR handout from your approved facts, and use the
topic list only to confirm you did not skip a category.
```

## Public domain and open licenses (when they're actually usable)

Some high-quality safe foundations exist:

- **Public domain / federal works.** Materials produced by U.S. federal
  government agencies are generally in the public domain in the U.S. — verify
  per item, check the site's policy, and confirm the work is a federal work.
- **Creative Commons–licensed materials.** Some organizations publish patient
  materials under CC licenses that allow adaptation with attribution. Read the
  license: "CC BY" (adapt with attribution) and "CC BY-SA" (share your version
  under the same license) are usable; "NoDerivatives" is not for adapting,
  and "NC/NonCommercial" needs a careful look at your facility's use.
- **Your facility's own library, with its usage rules.** Oftentimes the
  highest-quality source is what you already have — as long as you respect how
  they say you may use it.

Attribution done right, on the footer's Source line:

```text
Source: adapted from [Publisher]'s "[Title]" ([year]),
used under the Creative Commons CC BY 4.0 license; changed from the original.
```

The line "changed from the original" (recommended by many CC licenses) is short
and honest. Keep the facility workflow rule too: your facility approval still
applies before printing anything adapted under a license.

## AI-generated content and copyright: the honest version

A purely AI-generated block of text sits in a murkier copyright place than
human-created work: in the U.S., copyright law protects human creativity, and
purely machine-drafted material may not be protected the same way. For patient
handouts, this rarely matters for protection — and matters a lot for honesty
about sources. It is one more reason the manual's stance is: **AI drafts; you
and your facility own and verify the final thing.** The footer line "Drafted
with AI assistance; human-reviewed" already tells the truth about the process.

## Practical do/don't table

| Situation | Safe? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rewrite your own draft with AI | Yes | Your expression, your facts |
| Copy facility handout into new sheet | Per facility rules | Their template, their call |
| "Have AI reword" another org's sheet | No | Paraphrase of expression can infringe |
| Rebuild topics from another sheet's outline | Generally yes | Ideas/topics not protected; verify liberty with the same trace-and-source checks |
| Use a CC BY handout with attribution | Yes if license fits | Read the license terms |
| Copy a federal-agency sheet for reuse | Generally yes (U.S.) | Public domain; still verify and attribute the real source |
| Use a stock image from a photo site | Check license | Plane "free look" sites are the classic trap |

## One habit that future-proofs you

Keep a **source log** while you draft — three lines per handout:

```text
Source log: handout-foot-care-v3
- Facts: facility handout #4412 (rev. 2026-08)
- Outline reference: [Agency] page (viewed 2026-10-25)
- AI assist: rewrite only; no clinical facts introduced (session dated)
```

Six lines total, and you have answered the two questions that every copyright,
review, or update question boils down to: where did this come from, and what
did the machine contribute.
